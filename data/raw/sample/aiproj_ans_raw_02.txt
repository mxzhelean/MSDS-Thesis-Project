For an introductory undergraduate AI Ethics course, effective semester projects
connect abstract ethical frameworks to tangible, real-world systems that
students can audit or investigate without needing advanced programming skills,
while fostering critical moral reasoning. Here are five specific project topics
with focused research questions:

1. Algorithmic Representation in Generative AI Students can conduct a structured
qualitative audit of popular text-to-image models (such as Midjourney or DALL-E)
by prompting them to generate images of various professions, socioeconomic
roles, or cultural groups. Research Question: To what extent do commercial
text-to-image generators reinforce gender and racial stereotypes across high-
and low-status professions, and how effective are current developer guardrails
at mitigating these biases?

2. EdTech Surveillance and Student Autonomy Many universities use AI-driven
proctoring software or automated text-detection tools (e.g., Turnitin,
Honorlock). Students can analyze institutional policies alongside documented
false-positive rates for non-native English speakers or neurodivergent learners.
Research Question: How do automated academic integrity tools balance
institutional fairness against student privacy and accessibility, and do their
error rates render their deployment ethically unjustifiable?

3. AI Companions and Emotional Vulnerability With the rise of personalized
chatbots (e.g., Replika, Character.ai), questions arise regarding parasocial
attachment, consent, and the monetization of loneliness. Students can evaluate
terms of service, interface design ("dark patterns"), and documented user
testimonies. Research Question: Under what ethical conditions, if any, is it
permissible for commercial AI companions to simulate empathy and romantic
attachment, particularly when marketed to minors or emotionally vulnerable
users?

4. The Hidden Human Labor Behind "Autonomous" AI Large language models rely
heavily on outsourced data labeling and content moderation, often performed
under precarious conditions in the Global South. Students can examine
investigative labor reports and corporate transparency disclosures through
frameworks of distributive justice. Research Question: How do major AI
developers distribute the economic benefits and psychological harms of data
annotation across global supply chains, and what ethical obligations do they owe
to contracted workers?

5. Environmental Justice and AI Infrastructure Training and querying large
models require substantial electricity and freshwater for data center cooling,
frequently straining local municipalities. Students can review municipal
water-use agreements, energy grid reports, and corporate sustainability
disclosures for a specific regional facility. Research Question: When local
communities bear the water and energy burdens of hyperscale AI data centers
without receiving proportional economic benefits, what frameworks of
environmental justice should govern AI infrastructure siting?

Project Execution Tip: Encourage students to pair their chosen research question
with an explicit ethical framework—such as utilitarianism, Kantian deontology,
care ethics, or Rawlsian justice—to structure their analysis and formulate
actionable policy recommendations for developers or regulators.
