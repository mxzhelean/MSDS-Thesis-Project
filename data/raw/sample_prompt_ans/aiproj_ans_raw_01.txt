Here are six semester projects designed for introductory students. Each can be completed through public documents, published research, or a small, voluntary study—without building or deploying an AI system.

Facial recognition in public spaces
Research question: How do the privacy and consent policies of three cities that use facial recognition differ, and do they meaningfully address risks to people who are misidentified?
Compare city policies, civil-rights reports, and documented cases. Present a short policy comparison and recommend one safeguard, such as independent audits or limits on where the technology may be used.

AI résumé screening and fairness
Research question: What evidence do employers or vendors provide that automated résumé screening is fair to applicants from different demographic groups?
Review public vendor claims, employer notices, and relevant studies. Identify what evidence is missing and propose questions an applicant or regulator should ask. Avoid testing tools on real applicants’ personal data.

Generative AI and unequal access in education
Research question: Do university rules for student use of generative AI account for differences in access, disability, language background, and instructor expectations?
Compare the policies of three institutions. Optionally conduct a brief, anonymous survey with instructor approval and appropriate consent. Produce a policy checklist that promotes both academic integrity and equitable access.

Recommendation systems and political content
Research question: How clearly do one or two major social-media platforms explain how recommendations shape users’ exposure to political content?
Analyze platform explanations, user controls, and independent research. Assess whether users can understand and change what is recommended. Your final report might propose clearer controls or disclosures.

Automated decisions in public benefits
Research question: What accountability measures are in place when an automated system contributes to decisions about access to public benefits?
Choose one documented case and examine appeals, human review, transparency, and the consequences of errors. Use court records, government documents, and reputable reporting; distinguish confirmed facts from allegations. End with a simple accountability framework.

AI mental-health chatbots
Research question: How do selected mental-health chatbots communicate their limitations, protect user privacy, and respond to signs of crisis?
Compare publicly available terms, privacy notices, and safety guidance rather than seeking or simulating crisis advice. Evaluate whether the information is understandable and easy to find, then recommend improvements.

For any topic, students can frame the project around a defined case, a small set of comparison criteria, and a practical recommendation. A strong final submission might include a research report, a brief presentation, and an accessible summary for a non-specialist audience. Students should avoid collecting sensitive personal information and clearly state the limits of their evidence.
